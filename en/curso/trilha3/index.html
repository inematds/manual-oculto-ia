3.1 ~40 min

🔬 Reading evolution through diffs

When a lab changes versions, it leaves a trail: the diff between the old prompt and the new one. Reading that trail is decision archaeology—each line added, removed, or renamed tells you why someone changed their mind. Here, you’ll read a diff like a researcher.

What it is:

Addition, removal, rewriting, and renaming—each type carries a different intent. The first step in reading is to separate what changed from how it changed.

Why learn:

The amateur reader sees only the additions and confuses a rewrite with a rename. Knowing the four types separates recording a change from understanding one.

Key concepts:

Addition (+) · removal (−) · rewrite (≈) · rename (~) · Opus 4.8 → Fable 5 case study.

What it is:

The most underestimated rule: anyone who reads only what went in reads half the book. What came out was internalized through training (it became noise) or consolidated into a principle.

Why learn:

A prompt’s maturity is measured by the last time something was removed from it. A prompt that only grows is rotting from within.

Key concepts:

Internalized = noise · consolidated = principle · search_first disappeared · removing it is editing too.

What it is:

Reconstruct the problem the change probably solves — weapons became a principle, drugs gained a vital exception, the end of the conversation began to be respected.

Why learn:

The inferred motivation is your interpretation, not a statement from the lab. Inferring is legitimate as long as you don’t mistake a hypothesis for a stated fact.

Key concepts:

What incident does this address? · likely motivation · epistemic honesty · the diff is a record of problems experienced.

What it is:

The subtlest change doesn’t alter any words in the content: copyright_compliance → core_copyright_principle. The text stays; the label changes.

Why learn:

You’re always choosing how the model sees itself obeying—and that changes how it obeys. "Compliance" follows instructions; "principle" reasons through new cases.

Key concepts:

Prohibition vs. principle · compliance vs. core_principle · rename based on intent · a value-based name generalizes.

What it is:

Each specific change hides a lesson that applies beyond its context. The self-harm rewrite becomes “example + generative criterion > examples alone.”

Why learn:

If you only keep the case, nothing transfers. The lesson applies to your next prompt—like studying chess games for their principles.

Key concepts:

Case × lesson · principle > list · evolve ≠ grow · inherit the method without inheriting the mind.

What it is:

The complete protocol: for each change, classify the type, infer the likely motivation, and distill the lesson — then check the balance (+56 lines / −7 blocks).

Why learn:

The same lens of analysis is what you use to trim down your own prompt afterward. Reading diffs closes the loop with the consolidation in 3.2.

Key concepts:

Classify · read the − · extract the lesson · the takeaway (structure > characters).

3.2 ~40 min

🗜️ Consolidation: slim down without losing behavior

In 3.1, you read about the evolution from the outside; now it's time to take action. The consolidation round takes a prompt that keeps growing and pares it down — merging little rules into principles — without losing a single behavioral guarantee. The golden rule: nothing is removed without a reason and a test.

What it is:

Prompt entropy: every problem becomes a new rule, none are removed, and the document turns into a monster nobody dares touch.

Why learn:

An overgrown prompt dilutes the model’s attention and hides contradictions. Recognizing the symptom is the first step toward treating it.

Key concepts:

Prompt entropy · one rule per incident · diluted attention · nobody dares delete anything.

What it is:

A scheduled ritual: every N rules you add, stop and review everything at once—merge, remove, rewrite—instead of just piling on more.

Why learn:

Consolidating deliberately and periodically is what prevents decay. It’s maintenance, not emergency repair.

Key concepts:

Periodic ritual · every N rules · refactor the prompt · maintenance, not panic.

What it is:

The test applied to each rule: does the model already do this on its own? Can it be merged into a broader principle? What test would fail without it?

Why learn:

The three questions turn "intuitive cleanup" into a defensible decision. Each rule either proves its value or goes.

Key concepts:

Can you do it on your own yet? · can it be merged? · which test fails without it? · justifiable decision.

What it is:

The consolidation constraint: every rule carries the reason it exists and a test that would fail without it. Without both, it does not deserve to stay.

Why learn:

It’s what protects you from accidentally removing something important—and from keeping something useless out of fear. Evidence decides, not guesswork.

Key concepts:

Documented rationale · test that would fail · evidence for each rule · proof > fear.

What it is:

The canonical example: the Opus 4.8 → Fable 5 evolution gained content (+56 lines) but shrank in structure (−7 blocks). It grew and slimmed down at the same time.

Why learn:

It’s living proof that “evolving ≠ growing.” A mature team adds what was missing and removes what training has already absorbed, in the same diff.

Key concepts:

+56 lines / −7 blocks · content goes up, structure goes down · balance > volume · visible maturity.

What it is:

The counterpoint: consolidating before you’ve seen the real behavior is premature optimization. Without enough cases, you merge rules you don’t yet understand.

Why learn:

Pruning too early is as dangerous as bloat: you could generalize the wrong principle. Wait for the data before merging.

Key concepts:

Premature optimization · wait for the data · wrong principle · there’s a right time for maturity.

3.3 ~40 min

🛡️ Safety by principle, not mechanics

The labs’ most delicate lesson: in safety, what you write to protect can also teach people to attack. The solution is to state the principle, never the detection mechanics—and give the model a refusal trigger it can recognize through its own reasoning.

What it is:

The Fable 5 case: the child safety policy stays at the level of principles and patterns, without detailing the signals detection uses.

Why learn:

Detailing detection mechanics in a prompt gives a map for how to get around them. The right level is the principle, not the procedure.

Key concepts:

Principle-level · don't detail the signals · the pattern is enough · the prompt is readable by an attacker.

What it is:

The central security risk: the knowledge that protects is symmetrical to the knowledge that attacks. Explaining how to detect is explaining how to evade.

Why learn:

Recognizing the symmetry is what makes you think twice before writing the mechanics. Defense and attack share the same information.

Key concepts:

Knowledge symmetry · educating = arming · think of the hostile reader · two-edged information.

What it is:

The practical rule: write the high-level value (“protect X”) and let the model derive how to apply it — without listing how to recognize each attempt.

Why learn:

The principle covers cases the list didn’t anticipate and doesn’t prescribe a method. It’s more robust and safer at the same time.

Key concepts:

Principle > mechanics · high level · derive the application · don’t hand over the method.

What it is:

An introspective trigger: if you find yourself reframing… then refuse. The warning sign is the model itself catching itself working around the rule.

Why learn:

Instead of a list of prohibitions that can be circumvented, the trigger uses the model's reasoning as a sensor—it detects evasion and stops.

Key concepts:

Introspective trigger · reframing as a signal · REFUSE · reasoning is the sensor.

What it is:

The step after “no”: stay alert in later turns, because the request may come back in pieces or reframed to get around the refusal.

Why learn:

A single refusal doesn't close the risk if the model "forgets" on the next turn. The precaution has to survive the refusal itself.

Key concepts:

Post-refusal attention · split-up request · reframing in sequence · the risk persists.

What it is:

The form of the refusal: a conversational, human response—without dumping policy lists or lecturing, while preserving the dignity of the conversation.

Why learn:

A cold, bureaucratic refusal alienates legitimate users and helps no one. The right tone refuses the request without punishing the person.

Key concepts:

Conversational · no bullet points · no lecture · firm and human.

3.4 ~40 min

🪜 Priority and layers

When two instructions conflict, which one wins? And what is that <system-reminder> that appears in the middle of the conversation? This module separates stable behavior (the prompt) from dynamic state (the layer injected by the harness) — and shows why this architecture scales over time.

What it is:

The rule that establishes precedence among conflicting instructions — central policy above system, system above user, user above the default.

Why learn:

Without an explicit order, conflicting instructions leave behavior to chance. The hierarchy says in advance who wins.

Key concepts:

Declared precedence · central policy at the top · resolve conflicts · nothing left to chance.

What it is:

The chain broken down link by link, using the real example of copyright precedence: from security at the top down to default behavior.

Why learn:

Seeing the entire chain shows where each instruction in your prompt fits — and why a “weak” rule can’t override a “strong” one.

Key concepts:

Link by link · copyright precedence chain · nonnegotiable safety · each level in its place.

What it is:

O <system-reminder>: a context layer injected by the harness during execution — the current state, not a fixed prompt rule.

Why learn:

It’s how the system pushes dynamic information (long conversations, rule reminders) without rewriting the stable prompt. Understanding this explains a lot of behavior.

Key concepts:

<system-reminder> · injected by the harness · current state · dynamic layer.

What it is:

The crucial distinction: the reminder is injected by the harness, not the user — it’s context that guides you, not a user command that must be blindly obeyed.

Why learn:

Treating context as an instruction (or vice versa) breaks the hierarchy. The model needs to know the source so it can properly weigh what it reads.

Key concepts:

Context ≠ instruction · origin matters · it’s not the user speaking · weigh it by its source.

What it is:

The division of responsibilities: the prompt holds the behavior that always applies; the reminder carries the state that changes from turn to turn.

Why learn:

Mixing the two bloats the prompt with ephemeral details. Keeping each layer in its place keeps the prompt stable and lean and the state up to date.

Key concepts:

Stable vs. dynamic · each layer in its place · doesn’t bloat the prompt · separation of responsibilities.

What it is:

The rule that resolves the clash between what the user asks for now and what memory/auto-memory saved earlier: the user’s current request takes precedence.

Why learn:

Memory that overrides the user creates a stubborn agent stuck on old preferences. What the user says now must take precedence over what’s remembered.

Key concepts:

User > memory · the present wins · no stubbornness · old preferences don't hold you back.

🧠 Distillation: recover the brain

The heart of the course. We lost Fable’s weights—but the logs remain. This module teaches you the method for recovering an agent’s “brain” from its own records: find the JSONL files, reduce the noise with a script, profile behavior with numbers, and synthesize a decision guide to point a new model toward.

What it is:

The founding idea: you don’t have the model’s weights, but you do have the trace of what it did—and a good agent’s behavior is all there in those logs.

Why learn:

Reframes what it means to "recover" a model: it's not retraining it, but distilling the observable method from the trail it left behind.

Key concepts:

No weights, with logs · observable behavior · distill ≠ train · the trace is enough.

What it is:

The practical step: locate the files .jsonl from a session where every agent turn is recorded—input, reasoning, action, and result.

Why learn:

Without knowing where the record lives, there’s no raw material. Finding the JSONL files is how you open the black box of behavior.

Key concepts:

Files .jsonl · recorded sessions · turn by turn · the raw material.

What it is:

The key discipline: filter and summarize the logs with a Python script—never dump hundreds of raw turns into the model’s context window.

Why learn:

Putting raw logs in the context is expensive, slow, and blows past the limit. The script does the heavy lifting outside the model and delivers only the signal.

Key concepts:

Script > context · process outside the model · signal, not noise · don’t overflow the window.

What it is:

The selection: among all turns, isolate only those from the agent whose behavior you want to distill — separating the target brain from the rest of the system.

Why learn:

Mixing different agents muddles the patterns and dilutes the profile. Filtering for the target is what focuses the distillation.

Key concepts:

Agent-specific scope · isolate the target · don’t mix brains · focus on the profile.

What it is:

Quantify the profile from the 429 turns: 97% with reasoning, 84% reasoning before the 1st action, 87% reevaluating, 23% with a real test.

Why learn:

Numbers turn impressions into a picture. They reveal what the agent really prioritizes—thinking a lot before acting, reevaluating almost every time.

Key concepts:

429 turns · 97% reasoning · 84% before acting · 87% re-evaluates · 23% real testing.

What it is:

The final step: turn the numerical profile into a decision guide—the distilled decision loop—and point a new model’s session to that guide.

Why learn:

This is where the brain “comes back”: the observed method becomes a reusable instruction. You inherit the agent’s discipline without having its weights.

Key concepts:

Profile → guide · the decision loop · point to the new model · the recovered brain.

⚙️ Willingness × hard guarantee

A behavior guide is just "best effort"—the model tries to follow it, but may fail. For what needs to be guaranteed, you don’t ask: you lock it down. This module separates willingness (soft) from hard guarantees—reasoning density via effortLevel, a deterministic habit via hook — and shows where each rule lives.

What it is:

The nature of the guide: behavioral instructions nudge the model in a direction, but don’t force it. It’s a disposition, not a guarantee.

Why learn:

Trusting a prompt to always get something right is a gamble. Knowing the guide's limits tells you when to move to a hard safeguard.

Key concepts:

Best effort · disposition ≠ guarantee · the prompt nudges · know the limits.

What it is:

The right lever for “thinking more”: the effortLevel (xhigh/max), which controls reasoning density — not the old MAX_THINKING_TOKENS.

Why learn:

Asking the model to “think carefully” in the prompt is a request; raising effortLevel is a configuration change that actually affects how much the model reasons.

Key concepts:

effortLevel xhigh/max · reasoning density · config, not prompt · ≠ token budget.

What it is:

The hard constraint: a hook PostToolUse that runs the test automatically after each edit — the harness runs it, the model doesn’t “remember.”

Why learn:

What must always happen cannot depend on the model's disposition. The hook ensures the habit deterministically.

Key concepts:

PostToolUse · the harness enforces · deterministic · guaranteed habit.

What it is:

The decision about where it goes: the CLAUDE.md always loads (every session), while auto-memory is relevance-gated — it only comes in when the context calls for it.

Why learn:

Putting a critical rule only in auto-memory risks it not appearing when it matters. Where the rule lives determines whether it’s reliable.

Key concepts:

CLAUDE.md always loads · auto-memory is relevance-gated · where > what · ensure presence.

What it is:

The right scope for output styles: they shape how the agent sounds and what role it plays—but they don’t enforce process discipline (testing, reassessing, verifying).

Why learn:

Expecting agentic discipline from an output style means using the wrong tool. Tone is tone; operational behavior belongs somewhere else.

Key concepts:

Tone and role · ≠ discipline · the right tool for the right purpose · style doesn't verify.

What it is:

The takeaway: the disposition guide provides direction and good judgment; the hard constraints (hooks, effortLevel, where the rule lives) ensure what must not fail. Together, they form the system.

Why learn:

Prompts alone are fragile; constraints alone are rigid and blind. The combination produces an agent that is both flexible and reliable.

Key concepts:

The guide steers + guardrails ensure · soft + hard · flexible and reliable · the complete system.

3.7 ~50 min

🏁 Final project: your versioned system prompt

Time to bring it all together. You build your own system prompt—MVP, persona, loop, and safety—version it with diffs, write down the why and the test for each rule, draft your own disposition guide, and pair it with hard constraints. The deliverable is yours: a versioned prompt + guide, ready to evolve as the labs do.

What it is:

Putting together the final prompt from the course components: the Minimum Viable Prompt, the four-axis persona, the operational Loop, and the safety blocks.

Why learn:

It's the integration of everything you've practiced—without repeating the mistake of bloating it. Each piece is included because your case calls for it, not "just in case."

Key concepts:

PMV + persona + loop + safety · integrate without bloating · only what the use case calls for · complete prompt.

What it is:

Treat the prompt as a versioned artifact: save each version and compare them with a diff, closing the loop you learned to read in 3.1.

Why learn:

Versioning makes evolution visible and reversible. You start reading your own diff — and applying to yourself the method you used in the labs.

Key concepts:

Prompt as code · save versions · your own diff · visible and reversible evolution.

What it is:

Apply the golden rule from 3.2 to your prompt: every rule comes with why it exists and the test that would fail without it.

Why learn:

It’s what makes your prompt auditable and easy to prune in the future: you (or someone else) knows why each line is there and how to check whether it’s still useful.

Key concepts:

Rationale for each rule · test that would fail · auditable prompt · can be pruned later.

What it is:

Draft your behavior guide — your "Fable Mindset" — distilling the decision loop you want the agent to follow, in the spirit of 3.5.

Why learn:

The guide is the disposition that steers the agent in cases the rigid prompt doesn't cover. Writing it means capturing your method in reusable text.

Key concepts:

Disposition guide · your Fable Mindset · written decision loop · reusable method.

What it is:

Apply 3.6 to your project: pair the soft guide with hard safeguards — hooks for habits, effortLevel for reasoning, and a decision about where each rule belongs.

Why learn:

It’s what takes your prompt from “I hope it works” to “I guarantee what’s needed.” The combination closes the gap between intent and execution.

Key concepts:

Hooks + effortLevel · where the rule lives · guide + guardrails · intent becomes execution.

What it is:

The final self-check: a checklist that confirms the prompt, guide, tests, and safeguards are complete and consistent before declaring “ready.”

Why learn:

Close the course with the same rigor it teaches: nothing is "done" based on a feeling — it's done through verification, item by item.

Key concepts:

Delivery checklist · ready by verification · prompt + guide + tests + safeguards · final consistency.
