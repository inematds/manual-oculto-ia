MODULE 1.6 Track 1 — Fundamentals

🔄 The shift: from rule to principle

The world's best prompts never stop changing—but changing isn't just grow. When Anthropic went from Opus 4.8 to Fable 5, they added content and still removed entire blocks. This module teaches you to read that evolution: why a rule becomes a principle, why every removal tells a story, and why this is the leap that separates “following a prompt” from “thinking like someone who writes prompts.”

📋6 topics
⏱~40 min
🎯Basic → Intermediate
🌉Bridge to Track 2

Detailed content

1

The 2024 → 2026 trend: the tide has turned

In the early years of system prompts (around 2023–2024), everyone had the same instinct: each problem becomes a new rule. Did the AI mess up? Add a bullet. Did a weird case come up? Add another bullet. The result was prompts that only got bigger — lists with hundreds of “do” and “don’t” rules. Between 2024 and 2026, that started to turn around.

🔄 The shift in one sentence

The best prompts stopped being lists of rules that only keep growing and became sets of principles that get trimmed down. Instead of “one more bullet for each case,” the approach became: “what general principle would cover all these cases at once?”

It’s not just aesthetics. A prompt with 400 bullets is a place where the model is lost: the rules start contradicting each other, attention gets diluted, and behavior gets worse—not better. A well-written principle restores signal where the list has turned into noise.

✗ The 2024 reflex (it only grows)

  • ✗Something went wrong? Add a rule.
  • ✗No rule is removed — only added.
  • ✗The prompt becomes a swamp of bullets.
  • ✗More text = a bet that “more is better.”

✓ The 2026 reflection (consolidated)

  • ✓Something went wrong? Ask which principle applies.
  • ✓What the model already does on its own is removed.
  • ✓Redundant rules merge into one.
  • ✓Less text, more signal—and more robust.
💡

Why this happened

Two reasons. First, models have gotten better—a lot of what needed being stated became natural behavior (the model already does it). Second, labs discovered in practice that bloated prompts make AI worse. Putting the two together, the winning strategy stopped being "write more" and became "write the right principle and delete the rest".

2

The real case: Opus 4.8 → Fable 5

The best way to see this shift isn’t in theory—it’s in a real diff. When Anthropic evolved the system prompt from Opus 4.8 to Fable 5, someone compared the two versions line by line. What emerged is a whole lesson on how cutting-edge prompts mature. Look at the timeline above: each version is a distillation of the previous one.

🧱

Opus 4.6 → 4.7 → 4.8 — the maturation

Each version adds nuance AND cuts what has become outdated.

The Opus 4.6 → 4.7 → 4.8 → Fable 5 sequence is, in practice, a queue of distillations: from one version to the next, the team observes the behavior, names what improved, and rewrites the prompt. It’s not just “adding a feature”— it’s refining how the model thinks.

📏

The numbers from the latest leap (4.8 → Fable 5)

Opus 4.8 = 3.769 lines · Fable 5 = 3.825 lines.

Balance: +56 lines. It seems minor… until you discover that, along the way, 7 entire blocks were removed and a lot of new content was added. In other words, a lot more than 56 lines of new material came in — but almost as much old material was removed. The small number hides a major overhaul.

🔍

14 changes, 5 lessons

The annotated diff lists 14 concrete changes.

Token budget block added, weapons policy reduced to one functional criterion, child safety expanded from 5 to 7 rules, copyright renamed, three blocks deleted… Each item represents a decision. The next topics pick up the four most important for you — without needing to read technical English.

🗺️

How to read a diff (in one line)

A "diff" is just the list of what changed between two versions: what was added, what was removed, what was rewritten. Most people only look at what went in. The whole point, you'll see, is in what left.

3

"Evolving ≠ growing": +56 lines, but −7 blocks

Here’s the first big lesson, and it’s a little counterintuitive. We tend to think that “improving” means “having more.” More rules, more details, more text. But Fable 5 proves the opposite: it became better while threw something away. Evolving isn't growing — it's refining.

🌱 The idea that sticks

Removing is editing too. A prompt that only grows is rotting from within: every incident becomes a rule, none are removed, and within a few months the model is navigating a swamp of contradictory instructions.

A prompt’s maturity is measured by the the last time something was removed from it. If all you’ve done for a while is add more, that’s a sign you’re due for a cleanup round — not more rules.

These are three of the blocks that Fable 5 deleted from the Opus 4.8 prompt — copied exactly as they were (in English). They weren’t removed because they were wrong; they were removed because the model already does this on its own:

Removed — diff, item 2
<search_first>

"...must search before answering EVERY factual question." It became default behavior — the obsession was redundant.

Removed — diff, item 3
<default_stance>

"Claude defaults to helping..." The default stance was absorbed by another block — it didn't need to be said twice.

Removed — diff, item 4
<respond_without_
citing_system_prompt>

"...does not attribute its behavior to its system prompt." Saying "don't say X" has the opposite effect — it's better to fix the source.

4

The block’s name is an instruction

This is the subtlest and most brilliant change in the diff. In one of the blocks — the copyright one — Anthropic the rule barely changed any words. What it changed was the name of the block. And that alone already changes how the model thinks.

BEFORE (Opus 4.8) — ~line 1408
<claude_prioritizes_copyright_compliance>

"Compliance" = obedience. The model follows through in the cases on the list.

AFTER (Fable 5) — ~line 1343
<core_copyright_principle>

"Principle" = value. The model reasons from it in new cases no one anticipated.

Notice: the rule's text still says that respecting copyright is non-negotiable and takes precedence over almost everything. What changes is the label — from compliance (comply) with principle (principle). The name tells the model how to see yourself obeying: following a rule, or acting from a value. And that changes how it decides when the case isn’t on the list.

🏷️ The label shapes cognition

This applies to any instruction, not just copyright. Calling a section prohibitions or from principles, from constraints or from guarantees — each choice pushes the model into a different way of thinking.

You are always choosing how the model sees itself obeying. A name that speaks to value generalizes; a name that speaks to enforcement only covers the list. For you writing prompts: the block’s name is the first instruction the model reads—choose it based on intent, not enforcement.

5

Every removal is information

Put together what we’ve seen: blocks disappear, rules merge, names change. That’s where a skill almost no one practices comes from — read what was removed. When a lab removes an instruction, that isn’t a gap: it’s a message. Reading prompt diffs is an archaeology of decisions.

🧠 Reason A: it was internalized

The model started doing that on its own (through training). The rule became noise — repeating the obvious only steals attention from the parts that matter.

Example: <search_first>. Searching before answering became a factory habit, so the obsessive ordering was removed.

🔗 Reason B: it was consolidated

Several little rules that said the same thing were merged into a single principle. The removal didn't remove the behavior — it only removed the repetition.

Example: four micro-rules about tone ("no pet names," "no 'honestly/actually'"…) became a single "assume the person is a capable adult and treat them as such".

💡

If you only read what went in, you’re reading half the book

What left of a prompt tells as rich a story as what went in. Next time you compare two versions of any instruction—yours or a lab's—look first at the lines removed and ask: "did this come out because it's already automatic, or because it was absorbed into a larger principle?". The answer teaches you about design.

6

What this means for you: principle > rule

You don’t work at an AI company—so why does this matter? Because the same shift applies to any instruction you give an AI: a tool's prompt, an agent's rules, even the text you paste into a chat. Thinking in principles, not rules, is the leap that separates those who use Whose AI guide AI. And that’s exactly the bridge to Track 2.

🎯 Principle > rule, in practice

  • ① Before adding a rule, ask whether there’s already a principle. Instead of listing ten cases, look for the general statement that covers all ten at once. Less text, broader coverage.
  • ② Clean up, don't just add more. Every so often, reread your instructions and cut what the AI already does on its own. A good prompt shrinks as much as it grows.
  • ③ Name it by intent. How the model "sees" the rule changes how it applies it. Call what you want it to generalize a principle .

🧩Who thinks for rule

  • •Add a bullet for each thing that goes wrong
  • •Covers only the cases you managed to imagine
  • •Sticks with long prompts that contradict each other
  • •Gets stuck on the first new case it didn't anticipate

✦Who thinks for principle

  • ✓Write the general statement that covers many cases
  • ✓Covers even cases you’ve never imagined
  • ✓Keeps the prompt short and coherent
  • ✓Adapts on its own to the new case
