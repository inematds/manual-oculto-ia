🧱 Fundamentals

If you’ve never instructed an AI before, start here. No jargon, with everyday analogies and lots of real examples, you’ll discover the invisible piece that decides who the AI is: the system prompt. Let’s open this note up, look at the pieces that make it up, the patterns that give it shape and voice, the mistakes that ruin everything — and the shift from thinking in rules to think about principles.

1.1 ~30 min

🧭 What is a system prompt

Before you type anything, the AI has already read an invisible text that decides who it is. Learn about the part almost no one sees, without jargon and with everyday analogies.

What it is:

A set of instructions that reaches the AI before your first message and stays in effect for the whole conversation—its "operating mode," which you don’t see on screen.

Why learn:

Almost everything that frustrates or delights you about an AI starts here. Knowing the note exists is the first step toward no longer suffering through AI and starting to guide it.

Key concepts:

Invisible · comes first · stays connected to the entire conversation · is the adjustable part.

What it is:

Two comparisons: the script defines the character for the same actor; the manual defines how the employee serves customers. The system prompt is both at once.

Why learn:

These are the analogies you’ll carry through the entire course — change the script, follow the manual without reciting it. They anchor everything that comes next.

Key concepts:

The script defines the character · the manual defines the rules · same talent · works behind the scenes.

What it is:

Every conversation has three voices: system (the invisible director), user (you) and assistant (the AI responding).

Why learn:

You see only two, but the third controls everything. Knowing how to distinguish the voices is the basic vocabulary for reading any conversation with AI.

Key concepts:

system (director) · user (you) · assistant (the AI) · who wins the conflict.

What it is:

The two practical reasons for the note: consistency (the AI acts the same way with everyone) and safety (it knows what not to do).

Why learn:

Without a system prompt, AI would be unpredictable and dangerous. Understanding why explains why this text exists in every serious AI system.

Key concepts:

Consistency · security · predictable behavior · safeguards.

What it is:

The same AI, with two different notes, becomes two different things—we saw Grok as a "study buddy" and as a "medical advisor," verbatim.

Why learn:

It’s proof that the ticket is powerful—and adjustable. Watch out for the wrong conclusion: it changes behavior, not knowledge.

Key concepts:

Same model · different prompts · it’s the script · it’s not knowledge.

What it is:

Each AI's "style" starts with the first line of the prompt—we saw Cursor ("You are an AI coding assistant…") and Grok, for real.

Why learn:

That feeling that every AI has its own personality is real — and it’s the choice of whoever wrote the note, not just the model.

Key concepts:

Identity on the 1st line · where it operates · stance · is a choice made by whoever wrote it.

1.2 ~35 min

🧩 The parts of a prompt

Now that you know the note exists, let’s open it and look at the parts inside: identity, tone, tools, safety, priority—and how it all fits together.

What it is:

The part that says who the AI is, what it is (the model), and even how current its knowledge is (the knowledge cutoff), stated right at the opening.

Why learn:

Without an anchor, AI drifts—it becomes another persona or invents powers. Identity is the first piece and the only one present in 100% of prompts.

Key concepts:

Persona · base model · knowledge cutoff · mission scope.

What it is:

The part that defines the writing style: formal or casual, brief or detailed, with or without lists—the tone calibrated to the product.

Why learn:

It's the element that most changes the "feel" of using AI—and the one that most separates a pleasant chatbot from a robotic one.

Key concepts:

Formal vs. casual · prose vs. list · honesty · calibrated tone.

What it is:

The part that lists what the AI can do in the world: search, read files, run code—each action with its scope and conditions for use.

Why learn:

It’s what turns a chatbot that only talks into an agent that acts. A weak declaration makes the AI promise what it can’t do or ignore what it can.

Key concepts:

Action in the world · scope · conditions of use · agent vs. chatbot.

What it is:

The part that states refusals and limits, placed early to signal precedence: safety comes before usefulness.

Why learn:

A blunt prohibition is easy to work around. A rule with a reason generalizes to situations its author never anticipated — you can see that right here.

Key concepts:

Refusal · safety > usefulness · early stance · rule with a reason.

What it is:

The part that sets precedence when two instructions conflict: central policy > system > user > default.

Why learn:

Without a clear order, conflicting instructions leave behavior to chance—the prompt needs to say who wins.

Key concepts:

Precedence · central policy · conflict resolution · user > default.

What it is:

The way to organize the pieces: each block in a named "drawer" with an XML tag (<tone_and_style>, <tool_calling>), real ones from Cursor.

Why learn:

It’s what makes a long prompt readable and easy to maintain. The drawer’s name is already half the instruction.

Key concepts:

Named block · XML drawer · organization · the name already gives instructions.

1.3 ~35 min

🏗️ Structural patterns

The patterns that give the prompt its SHAPE—the skeleton that appears before any behavioral rule: identity, capabilities, XML container, rule by example.

What it is:

The first structural pattern: “You are X” + model + knowledge cutoff, seen in 100% of real prompts.

Why learn:

Identity is an anchor, not a biography — recognizing the pattern avoids the antipattern of a buried or bloated persona.

Key concepts:

Anchor · "You are…" · 100% universal · short and precise.

What it is:

The pattern of listing capabilities in a structured way—each with “what it does,” “when to use it,” and “limits.”

Why learn:

A clear list avoids both extremes: offering what doesn't exist and ignoring what does.

Key concepts:

Scope · "if the user…" condition · limits · anti-overpromising.

What it is:

The pattern of wrapping each logical block in a named XML tag—flat (few levels) and modular.

Why learn:

It’s what makes a prompt with thousands of lines maintainable and the diff between versions readable.

Key concepts:

Named block · flat · modular · diffable (Markdown works too).

What it is:

The pattern of pairing an abstract rule with a concrete demonstration (input → output)—like Perplexity’s citation format.

Why learn:

A well-chosen example reinforces behavior better than three paragraphs—the AI learns by imitation.

Key concepts:

Demonstration · input → output · imitation · rule + example.

What it is:

The pattern of choosing meaningful block names—the drawer’s title guides behavior; it doesn’t just organize.

Why learn:

A good name saves lines of rules and sets the stage for the turn in Module 1.6 (the block's name is an instruction).

Key concepts:

Meaningful name · guiding label · fewer rules · semantics.

What it is:

Cases where the XML compartment becomes too much ceremony: short prompts, code (where Markdown headers are enough).

Why learn:

Every pattern has a limit. Knowing when NOT to apply it is what separates those who copy from those who understand.

Key concepts:

Pattern limit · too much ceremony · short prompt · Markdown is enough.

1.4 ~40 min

🎚️ Behavioral patterns

The patterns that give the model its VOICE and JUDGMENT—how it acts, decides, and speaks: rule with a reason, Always/Never contrast, concision budget, and tool contract.

What it is:

The pattern of pairing every rule with the reasoning behind it, instead of a blunt command.

Why learn:

The rationale lets AI generalize to unforeseen cases, instead of obeying mechanically and failing at the edges.

Key concepts:

Explicit reasoning · generalization · edge-case robustness · intent over wording.

What it is:

The pair that sets the desired and prohibited behaviors side by side—we saw Cursor ("NEVER create files… ALWAYS prefer editing…").

Why learn:

The contrast removes the ambiguity in the middle: the AI knows exactly which pole to move toward and which to avoid.

Key concepts:

ALWAYS/NEVER · desired/forbidden pair · no middle ground · emphasis.

What it is:

The explicit instruction about the ideal response length—Grok ("You provide the shortest answer you can…").

Why learn:

Without a budget, the AI "pads the response"; with one, it calibrates density to the channel (terminal, chat, API).

Key concepts:

Target length · expand/cut · density by channel · anti-verbosity.

What it is:

Behavior rules for tools: when to call them, in what order, when to stop — the first look at a topic from Track 2.

Why learn:

Declaring the tool isn't enough — its usage contract is what prevents redundant, out-of-order, or unnecessary calls.

Key concepts:

When to call · order · prerequisite · when to stop.

What it is:

The idea that ALL CAPS, "MUST," and "CRITICAL" are currency: overspending devalues every rule.

Why learn:

When everything is shouting, nothing stands out. Treating emphasis as a scarce resource is what keeps the prompt audible—and anticipates Error 1 in Module 1.5.

Key concepts:

Emphasis as currency · inflation · scarce resource · audibility.

What it is:

The rule for using absolutes (always/never) only when there really are no exceptions — otherwise they clash and lose their force.

Why learn:

Too many absolutes create conflicts that are impossible to obey. Restraint is what keeps every “always” meaningful.

Key concepts:

True absolutes · restraint · avoid collisions · preserve strength.

🚫 The 5 mistakes that ruin a prompt

The five anti-patterns that sabotage the outcome—and the antidote for each. Before → after grids show how to go from a chaotic prompt to a clean one.

What it is:

Stuff the prompt with ALL CAPS, "MUST," and "CRITICAL" until the emphasis loses all its value.

Why learn:

It’s the most common beginner mistake. The antidote: treat emphasis as currency and save it for what really matters.

Key concepts:

Everything screams · emphasis loses its value · antidote: restraint · audibility.

What it is:

Adding rule after rule with each problem without ever removing the old ones—the prompt becomes layers of contradictory onion.

Why learn:

It’s how prompts bloat until they become unmanageable. The antidote: consolidate—remove and rewrite, don’t just add.

Key concepts:

Layers · internal contradiction · bloat · antidote: consolidate.

What it is:

Describe the exact mechanics of what to avoid (step by step) instead of the principle—a list that ends up teaching what it meant to prohibit.

Why learn:

It’s the seed of the security lesson in Track 3. The antidote: state the principle, not the recipe.

Key concepts:

Mechanics vs. principle · dangerous symmetry · antidote: the why · high-level.

What it is:

Put what should be current context (date, user, task) into the stable prompt — mixing what doesn't change with what changes each turn.

Why learn:

Confusing the layers leads to fragile prompts and wrong conclusions when reading a leak. The antidote: separate fixed from dynamic.

Key concepts:

Stable vs. dynamic · current context · harness · antidote: separate them.

What it is:

Specify every tiny step out of distrust, without letting the AI use its own judgment — instructions that suffocate instead of guide.

Why learn:

Micromanaging breaks generalization. The antidote: trust the AI’s judgment by giving it principles instead of step-by-step recipes.

Key concepts:

Micromanagement · stifled judgment · loss of generalization · antidote: trust.

What it is:

The wrap-up that brings the 5 errors and their antidotes together in a single table, with "emphasis is currency" as the guiding thread.

Why learn:

Seeing all five together turns a list of cautions into a mental checklist you can apply to any prompt.

Key concepts:

Emphasis is currency · 5 mistakes + 5 antidotes · mental checklist · synthesis.

🔄 The shift: from rule to principle

The field’s big shift between 2024 and 2026: fewer mechanical rules, more principles. We follow a real diff case—Opus 4.8 → Fable 5—and what it teaches us.

What it is:

The field’s change in direction: from prompts packed with rigid rules to prompts built on generalizable principles.

Why learn:

Understanding the trend puts you ahead — you start writing in the style the labs are moving toward, not what’s already outdated.

Key concepts:

Trend · rule → principle · field maturity · labs’ direction.

What it is:

A real diff between two versions of a lab prompt—the annotated comparison that shows the shift happening in practice.

Why learn:

Reading changes through diffs is the core skill of Track 3 — here you get your first look at it in a concrete, narrated example.

Key concepts:

Version diff · real case · annotated comparison · read the evolution.

What it is:

The case’s paradox: the prompt gained 56 lines but lost 7 rule blocks—it grew in explanation and shrank in mechanics.

Why learn:

It challenges the intuition that "bigger = better." The right direction is to consolidate rules into principles, even if the text gets longer.

Key concepts:

Evolve ≠ grow · +56 / −7 · consolidation · quality > size.

What it is:

In the real case, copyright_compliance became core_copyright_principle — renaming alone changes the AI’s stance.

Why learn:

From "follow a rule" to "honor a principle": the name carries the intent. Small change, big effect.

Key concepts:

Renaming instructs · compliance → principle · stance · intention in the name.

What it is:

The view that every block removed between versions reveals a decision—what was removed matters as much as what was added.

Why learn:

Beginners only look at what was added. Learning to read deletions is half the skill of interpreting a diff.

Key concepts:

Removal = information · decision revealed · read what was removed · double signal.

What it is:

The practical takeaway: when writing or adjusting a prompt, always prefer a principle that generalizes over a rule that covers only one case.

Why learn:

It's the compass that closes Track 1 and opens Track 2—you'll be ready to get hands-on with the right mindset.

Key concepts:

Principle > rule · generalization · practical compass · bridge to Track 2.
